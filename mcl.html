Monte Carlo Localization

A particle filter estimates where a robot is by scattering guesses, scoring each against the sensors, and resampling toward the good ones. This paper asks a narrower question: how does the count of guesses trade off against the error that is left, and where does adding more stop being worth it.

The question

On a VEX field the robot starts an autonomous routine not fully certain where it is, and that uncertainty compounds through the run. Monte Carlo Localization handles it with a cloud of N particles, each a hypothesis for the pose (x, y, θ). More particles cover the space better but cost compute, so somewhere there is an optimal N.

I modelled the relationship between N and the mean positional error, and then tested how that relationship shifts as the field gets more cluttered.

The math

Why the error goes as 1 / √N

Each particle is scored by how far its predicted sensor reading sits from the real one, squared to punish large misses:

ei = (dfront tested − actual)² + (dright tested − actual)²

That error is pushed through a Gaussian so weight falls off smoothly with distance, then all weights are normalised to sum to one:

wi = e−ei / 2σ²  →  wi' = wi / Σ wj

The particles are resampled in proportion to wi' so good ones get copied and bad ones drop out, and the estimate is the average of what survives. The error for a trial is the distance from that average to the true position.

The estimate is a mean of N samples, so its variance is σ²/N and its spread is σ/√N. That spread is exactly what the error measures, so the error has to follow:

E(N) = k / √N + c

k sets how fast the error falls; c is the floor it cannot beat. Substituting x = 1/√N makes this a straight line, so linear regression on the trial data recovers k and c. The fit came back with a strong Pearson correlation, so the inverse–square–root model holds.

Mean localization error in metres, averaged over 1000 randomised trials.

Across all three layouts k came out roughly the same: the rate at which particles buy down error does not care about the environment. What changes is c, the floor.

No obstaclesFloor near 0.158 m. Even a plain square has poses where both sensors read the same wall.
Center pillarFloor near 0.465 m, the worst. The pillar acts as a second set of walls, so more positions give identical readings and the estimate collapses toward the middle.
4 cornersFloor near 0.125 m, the best. The boxes break up the long walls the ambiguous particles spread along, and are too small for both sensors to catch at once.

So past a few hundred particles the fight is no longer against randomness, it is against the geometry of the room. On this field roughly 400 to 750 particles captured almost all of the accuracy that was available.
